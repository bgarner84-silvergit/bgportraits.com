# Fashion sports portraits, and what to steal from them

Written 2026-09-20, following `dfw-market-research.md` and
`senior-portrait-bundle-analysis.md`. Prompted by Brandon's question about Jeff Dietz's
"Fashion Sports Portraits," plus his stated principle: **charge more when the value is
genuinely higher, even when the added value costs little to deliver.** That principle is
correct and is the organizing idea of this document.

---

## What the product actually is

Jeff Dietz Photography (Dallas, serves Highland Park and Southlake, roughly 20 years in
business, self-described "#1 sports portrait photographer in DFW") calls itself "home of the
fashion sports portrait." The product:

- **Editorial styling applied to an athlete.** Explicit references: GQ, Vanity Fair, Sports
  Illustrated. The stated goal is that the senior "looks like they had just been photographed
  for a celebrity magazine."
- **Wardrobe is the mechanism.** Two to three looks per session: suits, fashion-forward
  casual wear, and team apparel. The athlete is photographed as a person of stature who
  happens to play a sport, not as a uniform.
- **Locations centered on the sport**, with the option to shoot the athlete entirely out of
  athletic context.
- **Deliberately light on Photoshop.** His own description: "rather than heavy photoshop
  effects," the studio delivers "classic" looks. The craft is in-camera.
- **Structure:** 2-hour on-location shoot, wardrobe guidance, posing direction, retouching on
  purchased images. $690 + tax session fee, includes $500 of image credit, followed by an
  in-home ordering appointment where the real spend happens.

## The strategic read: do not try to be this

Dietz sells **status transfer**: your kid looks like a celebrity. bgportraits sells a
**commissioned object**: one piece, built, framed, on the wall. Those are different products
even though they photograph the same kid in the same week.

Three reasons not to chase his version:

1. **It competes on his strength using a skill we do not have.** The fashion look is
   in-camera craft: wardrobe direction, posing across multiple registers, lighting for
   editorial rather than drama. That is the same skill gap flagged in
   `senior-portrait-bundle-analysis.md`, and it is what twenty years buys him.
2. **It explicitly avoids the thing that makes our economics work.** His differentiator is
   *not* heavy post. Ours is entirely post: the composite is the product, executed by an
   outside editor at roughly $100 all in. Competing on in-camera fashion craft throws away
   the leverage.
3. **It prices where we decided not to be.** $690 plus an ordering appointment puts him
   squarely in the $690 to $1,500 band the DFW research mapped. That is a real business, and
   it is below the floor the commission ladder was built to clear.

## What to steal: the register, not the method

Here is the arbitrage, and it is the whole point of this document.

**Dietz buys look variety with shoot time and styling skill.** Two to three wardrobe changes
across a two-hour session, plus locations, plus the craft to light and pose each one
differently. Expensive in exactly the resource that is scarcest for a solo operator with 5 to
10 hours a week.

**We can buy look variety in post, at roughly $100 a composite.** The board already
established this when the production model was corrected: composites stopped being scarce
hours and became vendor cash, and the 1-to-8 composite span collapsed from 14.0 owner hours
to 3.5. What has not been exploited yet is *what those composites should be*.

Today the flagship's five composites are, per the cost model, "composite #1 is the hard one,
every composite after it is a variant built off that first one." Five variants of one idea.
A parent looking at that sees one picture, five times, and the fifth one adds almost nothing
to what they feel they are buying.

The same five composites, built as **five different registers**, cost the same and land
completely differently:

| Register | What it does for the family | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| The hero composite | The wall piece. The commission. Already the flagship. | ~$100 |
| The editorial / magazine frame | The Dietz register, built in post from a static frame. Athlete as person of stature. | ~$100 |
| The black-and-white fine-art frame | The quiet one. Already scoped as a deferred line (`project-brief.md` decision 13). | ~$100 |
| The recruiting / signing-day asset | For the athlete, not the parent. Social crops, something he posts. | ~$100 |
| The family / grandparent frame | Clean, warm, un-stylized. Pairs with the bonus set in `senior-portrait-bundle-analysis.md`. | ~$100 |

Each one has a different job, a different audience, and a different place in the house. That
is genuinely more value, not more volume, and it costs what the current five variants
already cost.

## The one change to the shoot that unlocks most of this

**Ask the athlete to bring one non-uniform look.** Ten to fifteen minutes of session time,
zero cash.

Everything above except the hero composite improves substantially with one wardrobe change
in the can. The editorial register in particular is hard to build convincingly from uniform
frames alone, because the entire point of Dietz's version is the athlete out of uniform. One
extra look in the same one-hour session is the cheapest thing on this list and unlocks the
most.

Pair it with a **written wardrobe and preparation guide**. Written once, reused on every
client forever, marginal cost zero. The luxury senior tier ($2,000 to $5,000 nationally)
uses exactly this, some running 90+ pages, alongside hair and makeup and multiple locations
we should not try to match. It is also self-serving: better wardrobe going in means better
raw material coming out, which means fewer vendor round trips and a better finished piece.

## The honest flag, and it is a real one

Hormozi's note survived the production-model correction and applies directly here:

> "One athlete. One portrait. One legacy." sold as five composites is still incoherent, and
> **cheap composites make that more dangerous, not less, because the cost discipline that
> used to restrain image stuffing is gone. The restraint now has to be deliberate and held
> in the copy.**

That warning was written about exactly this move. The distinction that keeps it honest:

- **Image stuffing** is five variants of one concept, sold as "you get five." It is quantity
  padding, it dilutes the headline, and a parent can feel it.
- **Register variety** is five pieces with five jobs, where **one of them is the commission**
  and the rest are the supporting set.

The structural rule that resolves it: **the hero piece stays singular and stays the pitch.**
One athlete, one portrait, one legacy, framed, on the wall. The other registers are delivered
the way the Family Set, the Digital Legacy Pack and the Certificate of Provenance already
are: generous inclusions discovered in the package, never the reason the price is what it
is. The moment the headline becomes "five pieces," the positioning that carries $3,500 is
gone and we are selling volume against people who sell volume better and cheaper.

This is the same discipline recommended in `senior-portrait-bundle-analysis.md` for the
senior-portrait bonus, and for the same reason. The pattern is now consistent across three
analyses: **add value in the package, never in the pitch.**

## Recommendation

1. **Change what the five composites are, not how many there are.** Build them as distinct
   registers rather than variants of one concept. Same cost, materially more value.
2. **Add one non-uniform wardrobe look to the session.** Ten to fifteen minutes, free,
   unlocks the editorial register and improves three of the five.
3. **Write the wardrobe and preparation guide once.** Zero marginal cost per client, improves
   input quality, and it is a visible artifact of a premium process.
4. **Keep the hero piece singular in all copy.** The supporting registers are inclusions, not
   the offer. Hold the restraint deliberately, because the cost model no longer holds it for
   us.
5. **Do not build the in-camera fashion product.** It is Dietz's strength, our weakness, and
   it prices below our floor.
