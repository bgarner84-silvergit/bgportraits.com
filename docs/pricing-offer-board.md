## Where they genuinely disagreed

### 1. The floor itself. This is the sharpest unresolved conflict.

Hormozi built his floor on the cost sheet's $100/hr and got **$1,487** for the flagship
stack, making $3,500 a comfortable 2.35x.

Economics rejected the basis outright: $100/hr is a **target wage**, not a required
billing rate. Loaded for self-employment tax, utilization (only ~55% of Brandon's hours are
delivery hours), plus the gear, insurance, mileage, marketing and travel time the sheet
excluded, the true rate is **~$280/delivery-hour** and the framed-tier floor is **$3,300**.

**Consequence: under Economics' basis, $3,500 is roughly break-even, not a 2.35x margin.**
Hormozi's ladder still works because his tiers scale on vendor cash rather than hours, but
the comfort in his numbers is an artifact of the cheaper rate. Treat $3,500 as the floor
for a **one-composite** piece, not as a healthy margin.

Economics also found three real errors in the cost sheet:

- **Stripe is charged against the current selling price, not the floor.** Circular. The
  gross-up method (`P = (subtotal + 0.30) / 0.971`) raises each floor by $12 to $35. Small,
  but it only ever errs in one direction.
- **The software divisor is wrong.** The sheet used "~4 sessions/month (placeholder)."
  Realistic throughput is ~1.5/month, so every per-session overhead allocation is about
  2.7x too low.
- **Travel time is not charged at all.** A local DFW shoot is realistically an hour of
  round trip drive. That is a 29% increase in fixed labor the sheet treats as free.

### 2. The ceiling on the current positioning

Dunford: the permanence and archival frame is load-bearing to roughly **$1,200 to $1,500**
and no higher. Above that the conservation close inverts and becomes a liability, because
it hands the buyer checkable arithmetic: *"I can get museum framing for $400, what is the
other $3,100 for?"*

This reads as a head-on collision with the $3,500 number until you notice Hormozi said the
same thing from the offer side: the anchor category decides the expected price before you
say a number, and the first sentence on the page has to place you in commissioned art
rather than photography.

**Resolution: not a contradiction, a precondition.** $3,500 is defensible; "a really nice
framed picture of my kid" at $3,500 is not. The repositioning is what makes the arithmetic
sellable.

### 3. Is 60 days enough?

- Economics: achievable even at 5 hrs/week, **but only** if the Grand Slam is capped at one
  composite. At five composites it is arithmetically impossible.
- Hormozi: the goal needs ~50 hours against 40 available at the low end. **Impossible at 5
  hrs/week**; needs ~6.25 hrs/week sustained for eight straight weeks with no missed week.
- Sandler: possible, but only via a warm channel. Puts Brandon's plan at ~30% and an
  inverted sequence at ~60%.

They agree on the shape: it is tight, and the binding constraint is about one hour a week
of slack, not the number of sessions.

## The recommended offer

Synthesized from Hormozi's architecture with Economics' floor discipline applied.

### Flagship: The Legacy Commission, $3,500

One athlete. One concept, art directed for him alone. One hand built composite. One piece,
framed to conservation standard.

The stack, with real cost to deliver:

| Component | Cost to Brandon |
|---|---|
| The commission: consult, art direction, shoot, scheduling, revisions, delivery, admin (3.5 hr) | $350 |
| The hero composite: hand cut, art directed scene, graded, retouched (3 hr + $70) | $370 |
| The piece: framed archival print, cotton rag, UV/AR glazing, reversible mounting | $300 `[ESTIMATE]` |
| Bonus, the Family Set: ten archival 8x10s for grandparents, locker, recruiting packets | $50 |
| Bonus, the Digital Legacy Pack: cutout PNG, AI background instructions, social crops | $25 |
| Bonus, Certificate of Provenance: signed, numbered, concept notes, archival spec, care | $40 |
| Bonus, The Reveal: in person unveiling and hand delivery (1 hr) | $100 |
| Bonus, The Archive Guarantee: master kept permanently, reprints at cost forever | $50 provision |
| Concept Exclusivity: the concept is locked against every other family on the roster, in writing | $0 |
| Software, Stripe | $25, $102 |

Total owner time: **8.0 hours**. Actual cash out of pocket: **$562**.

**The Concept Approval Guarantee is the highest-leverage element in the whole design**, and
it is nearly free because the expensive hard cost is incurred *after* approval:

1. **Commission.** $1,250 deposit books the slot, covers the shoot and the concept.
2. **Proof.** The finished composite is delivered watermarked. Don't love it, Brandon
   rebuilds it once free. Still don't love it, the commission ends and **the deposit is
   refunded in full.**
3. **The Piece.** Only on written approval does the $2,250 balance get collected and the
   framing get ordered.

Said to a parent: **"You don't pay for the portrait until you've seen the portrait."** That
is the entire sale, and it requires no sales skill. Worst case for Brandon is his own time
plus $70 of materials, because the frame was never ordered.

### The ladder

The structural rule: **every tier contains exactly one composite and exactly 3 Photoshop
hours.** Tiers are built by removing artifact and experience, never by removing images.
Margin per Photoshop hour then rises with price by construction.

| Tier | Price | Margin / Photoshop hour |
|---|---|---|
| **The Family Edition** | $5,300 | $1,037 |
| **The Legacy Commission** (flagship) | $3,500 | $671 |
| **The Framed Portrait** | $1,950 | $243 |
| ~~The Digital Commission~~ | ~~$1,250~~ | $116, **do not publish** |

Today's ladder runs **$37 → $36 → $19** per scarce hour. It gets worse the more a customer
spends. The proposed one runs strictly upward and cannot be inverted without adding
composites, which the design forbids.

**The Family Edition is the most important economic fact in the packet.** It is the
flagship plus two additional framed pieces. A duplicate framed piece consumes **zero
Photoshop hours and zero session hours**: it is hard cost plus about fifteen minutes of
ordering. Sold standalone it is $900 against $300 of cost. The correct direction to extend
this business is **more frames of the same image, never more images.** Its main job,
though, is to be the anchor that makes $3,500 read as the sensible middle.

**Kill the $1,250 tier as a published price.** It clears its floor but fails on opportunity
cost: it consumes a full session out of very few slots, and next to $1,950 on a price page
cannibalization is the likely case, not the edge case. Hold it in your pocket as a save,
quoted only after a prospect has explicitly declined $1,950.

**Additional composites: price them to be declined.** At the flagship the break-even
against margin-per-hour is absurd, so variants are **not available at Tier 1 at any
price**. Below it, $750 each. Their real function is to make "one portrait" feel like the
right answer, which it is.

### Founding Families

Converged range across seats: **$1,495 to $1,750, two to three seats.** Recommend
**$1,750, three seats, the full flagship deliverable with nothing removed.**

Two reasons for the full deliverable. It still clears the cost-sheet floor, so Brandon pays
himself and keeps ~$314. And proof of a $1,950 tier does not sell a $3,500 tier: he needs
photographs of the reveal, the certificate, and the big framed piece on a wall.

**At $399 he would lose $275 in actual cash per client** and arrive at the full-price
conversation having publicly valued the work at a tenth of what he is about to ask.

Contractual, signed before the shoot, in order of value:

1. Unlimited likeness and image release, perpetual, including paid advertising.
2. **Filmed reveal.** The single most valuable asset in the deal. A parent's unrehearsed
   reaction does the category-creation work at awareness 0 that Brandon cannot do in words
   and is not comfortable doing out loud.
3. Structured written testimonial within 14 days, minimum 75 words, answering three
   prompts: what were you going to do instead and why did you almost not do this, what
   surprised you, where does it hang and what happens when people see it.
4. Public Google review within 14 days. Searchable; a testimonial is not.
5. **Three warm introductions by name** within 30 days, with a text intro Brandon drafts
   for them. "Tell your friends" produces nothing.
6. **Recorded 20 minute feedback call**, including: *what would you have paid if I hadn't
   offered you the founding price?* This is the pricing research that does not otherwise
   exist, which is why it is a contract term and not a nicety.
7. Consequence clause: if 3 to 5 are not delivered in 30 days, the balance becomes due.

Anti-anchoring, all five required: the invoice shows **$3,500** with a separate
**"Founding Family portfolio credit, $1,750"** line; it is date stamped "Season One, Fall
2026" so it cannot recur; it is **never listed on the website**; seat count and expiry are
published on the invitation; and the exchange is itemized so the parent understands it is a
trade, not a market price.

## The sales process, for a self-described non-salesman

Sandler's verdict: **he cannot sell it as he operates today, and the reason is almost
nothing to do with charisma.** Four things break, in this order:

1. **Qualification.** There is no gate anywhere. The first time price comes up, Brandon is
   talking to someone he knows nothing about. That is where a non-salesman discounts or
   quotes the safe cheap tier. Highest probability failure, and it happens on inquiry one.
2. **The second parent.** A $3,500 discretionary purchase in a two-parent household is
   never a one-person decision, and nothing in the process convenes the second decider.
   Best realistic outcome of a good conversation becomes "let me talk to my spouse," which
   is a no that gets misfiled as a maybe and chased for three weeks.
3. **Follow-up.** No deposit, no dated next step. Polite apologetic follow-up signals the
   price is negotiable.
4. **The price conversation.** Brandon's actual worry, and the **least** of his four
   problems. Almost entirely solvable by structure.

**What removes the need to be good at selling**, in order of leverage:

1. **The price goes in writing from the bot before any call, always.** He never says a
   number first and never watches a face when it lands. One afternoon in GoHighLevel.
2. **A stated capacity limit that is true.** Declining business is the most persuasive
   thing an unproven seller can do, and a real constraint lets him do it without acting.
3. **A deposit that filters.** The $150 in the current GHL plan is right for $599 and wrong
   for $3,500: too small to filter. Use the staged $1,250 commission deposit above.
4. **Permission to disqualify, stated up front.** *"At the end I'd ask for a yes or a no.
   Both are completely fine. The only answer that's hard for me is 'let me think about
   it.'"* Get a verbal "fair" at the start of the call and cash it at the end. **This is the
   whole Sandler answer for a non-salesman, and it is free.**
5. The portfolio persuades, not the person. Send the work 24 hours before the call with
   "don't reply to this, just look at it before we talk."
6. Scripts he reads off a second monitor, not memorizes. Improvisation is where
   non-salesmen lose.
7. Written no-discount policy on the proposal. A policy holds under pressure; resolve does
   not.
8. A dated next step on every lead. *"I'll text you Thursday at noon for a yes or a no. If
   I don't hear back I'll take that as a no."* That sentence is the entire follow-up system,
   and it means he never has to chase.

**What cannot be made charisma-free:** the pain funnel. Someone has to ask a parent
uncomfortable questions and then be quiet. Sandler's note is that this is a behavior rather
than a talent, and that Brandon already does exactly this for a living as the translator
between business and technical teams. That skill transfers directly.

**Segment to target:** parents of **seniors, or of athletes whose last season is ending
now.** It is the only segment where the deadline is external and dated. Everyone else is a
hobby lead with future-tense, non-urgent regret.

## Positioning: the precondition

Dunford's finding, which Hormozi independently corroborated from the offer side.

**"Uniqueness" is not a differentiated capability, it is a category attribute.** Every
photograph of a child is unique, including the team photographer's.

**"Permanence" is verifiable and purchasable elsewhere, which is the worst combination at a
premium price.** Cotton rag, UV glazing, reversible mounting: a parent can price every line
item at a frame shop. At $849 that reads as generous. At $3,500 the conservation close
hands the buyer the arithmetic that kills the sale.

**At $3,000+ the competitive set leaves photography entirely.** The real competitor is the
rest of the youth-athlete wallet: club dues, travel, camps, private coaching, recruiting
and highlight services. That budget is already open, already four figures, already
justified by the same emotion. It is a *recognition and investment in my kid* budget, not a
home decor budget, and the current positioning speaks to decor.

**The theme that can carry the price: commission, not preservation. Authorship, not
ownership.** The genuinely differentiated capability is that this is **not a photograph**.
The camera captured a person; everything else in the image was decided. That is the
definition of commissioned work, and it is the one honest reason a single image of one
child can cost several thousand dollars, because in that category it already does.

Three things this fixes at once:

1. It borrows a category that already prices in four figures. The parent has no mental
   price for "athlete portrait" but does have one for "commissioned portrait of my child."
2. It demotes archival framing from the close to a proof point, where it stops being
   arithmetic bait.
3. **It chooses the premium category whose proof requirements Brandon can actually satisfy
   today.** Commission markets run on portfolio and artist. Nobody asks a portrait painter
   for testimonials. The heirloom wall-art studio category runs heavily on reviews and
   referrals, which is exactly the proof he does not have. This is the strongest single
   argument in the run.

**Naming.** "The Framed Piece" is the worst name on the current ladder at a premium price:
it tells the buyer the top tier's distinguishing feature is the frame, the one component
she can independently price at $400. Name the top tier for the **engagement**, not the
object.

**What must exist before four figures is sellable at all**, in priority order: the
**before/after** (raw frame beside the finished piece, large, above the fold, which proves
"Built, not taken" instead of asserting it and creates the category without naming a
competitor); one sentence of plain category language high on the page; the framed piece
photographed **on an actual wall next to furniture**, because scale is the cheapest premium
signal that requires no clients; and repricing the live tiers now, because the site is
currently teaching the market the wrong number and a founding price only reads as a favor
if it sits below a credible published one.

## Sequence: three seats overruled the stated plan

Brandon's plan was Grand Slam first, Founding Families later. Sandler, Hormozi and
Economics independently inverted it. Founding Families is not mop-up; it is the only
mechanism inside 60 days that manufactures the proof the premium sale requires.

**Week 1.** Get the framing vendor quote **and lead time**. Write the agreement (release,
filming consent, testimonial spec, consequence clause). Publish the three prices. Stripe
live, retainer link, e-sign template. Build the warm list: 20 direct approaches to parents
of seniors and last-season athletes via coaches, boosters, the existing client, personal
network.

**Weeks 1 to 3.** Fill and shoot all three Founding seats, **in the same week** so the
composites batch.

**Weeks 3 to 5.** Deliver. **Capture the testimonial and the filmed reaction at the digital
proof reveal, not at the framed delivery.** Some drama is lost; two to three weeks are
gained, and framing lead time would otherwise push the proof past the point where it can
help. The deliverable of this phase is not portraits, it is three filmed testimonials,
three framed-on-the-wall photographs, and six introductions.

**Weeks 5 to 7.** Prospect full price with finished pieces and filmed reactions in hand.
Run the full process: written price, both parents, fit call, staged deposit.

**Week 8.** Every open lead gets a yes or a no. Nothing carries into December ambiguous.

### Two scheduling facts that change the calendar

**Shoots are season-perishable; editing is not.** Front-load every shoot before
mid-November and composite into December. But this only converts to season revenue if
Brandon **bills at booking** rather than at delivery.

**The real booking cutoff is driven by framing lead time, not by the season.** At a
four-week lead time the last bookable shoot for framed holiday delivery is roughly
**October 20**, not mid-November.

### Realistic revenue

| Scenario | Mix | Gross | Cash kept |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optimistic, 10 hrs/wk held | 3 founding + 1 full | $8,750 | ~$6,655 |
| Realistic, 6 to 7 hrs/wk | 2 founding + 1 full | $7,000 | ~$5,416 |
| Downside | 2 founding, no full price | $3,500 | ~$2,478 plus the proof assets |

Any plan assuming more than four clients this season is fiction. Economics independently
landed in the same band at $4,500 to $7,000.

## Open risks

- ⚠️ **The framing vendor quote was deferred indefinitely on 2026-09-16. That deferral is
  now a live risk, and for a reason nobody anticipated: lead time, not price.** Hormozi
  stress-tested the $300 estimate to $700 and the offer survives. A six-week lead time
  breaks the season plan outright. Get the quote in week one for the calendar, not the
  budget.
- ⚠️ **$3,500 is unvalidated.** All four seats named this as the one thing that would change
  their answer. It cannot be derived, only observed.
- ⚠️ **The revision policy is still undefined**, and at 1 to 2 clients a month it is a
  capacity risk rather than a cost risk. One client demanding three rounds plausibly
  consumes 25 to 50% of a month's entire delivery budget. Premium buyers ask for more, not
  less. Lock it before launching a premium price.
- ⚠️ **The overhead allocation is circular**: per-session gear, insurance, software and
  marketing all depend on annual session count, which is the unknown. At 6 sessions/year
  those lines roughly triple. Economics calls this the largest soft spot in the cost
  structure, larger than the framing estimate.
- ⚠️ **Do not publish an unverified anchor.** The "$3,000 to $10,000 commissioned oil
  portrait" comparison is an assumption. A parent who fact-checks it destroys the premium
  frame in one click.
- ⚠️ **If the real ceiling turns out to be ~$800 to $1,200**, no ladder restructuring saves
  this. The correct response is not to cut price but to **change the buyer** to
  recruiting-track, private school, select and club families where the spend is already
  normalized. Failing that, the honest answer is the one already recorded: photography is
  the proving ground for the CRM and AI agent stack, not the income vehicle.

## Two questions the board needs answered

1. **Is there a warm channel?** A coach, booster club, team parent group, or the existing
   client's network. Sandler's entire verdict flips on this. With one, a referred premium
   sale is a different transaction: the proof problem is solved by the referrer and the
   close needs a calendar and a deposit link rather than charisma. Without one, the 60 day
   goal is being pursued through cold awareness-0 web traffic by an unreviewed solo studio
   charging thousands, and no sales process rescues that.
2. **What did the existing client think she was buying?** One phone call. If she bought a
   **keepsake**, the commission frame is right. If she bought **recognition** (the image
   made her son look like the athlete he believes he is), then the value theme is
   recognition rather than artistry, the competing budget is the recruiting line rather
   than the decor line, the proof is the athlete's reaction rather than the material
   spec, and permanence should be cut further than recommended here.

## Two new offer elements, from the correction

**The Master File.** The purchased layered PSD should be a named deliverable, not a
production detail. A flattened export degrades as a legacy asset: fixed size, baked-in
grade, unrecoverable. A layered master can be re-output at any size, re-graded and
re-printed in thirty years. It turns "re-framable in 30 years" from a framing claim into an
asset claim. And it does the category work nothing else can: **the team photographer
licenses you a download; a commission transfers the work.**

**The Concept Session, $250, credited in full against any commission.** This is the
direct answer to Brandon's stated worry that he can deliver but cannot sell. It converts
"sell a parent a $3,500 portrait" into "sit down and design your son's portrait together,
on screen, feature by feature." He is art directing, which he is good at, rather than
pitching, which he is not. **The consult becomes the close without feeling like one.** It
also qualifies out tire kickers and is the fastest available source of willingness-to-pay
data: a parent who pays $250 sight unseen tells you more than any survey.

Also: deliver the **annotated brief itself** inside the Certificate of Provenance, signed
and dated. It costs nothing, it already exists as a work product, and it is provenance in
the art-world sense. *"This is the brief I wrote for your son's portrait."*

## The disclosure question

Execution is commissioned out. **Do not conceal it.** If a parent discovers it after paying
$3,500, the premium dies and takes the referrals with it, and in a closed private-school
network it takes the channel too.

Hormozi's fix is to state it as a credential in the Certificate of Provenance: *"Art
direction and finishing by Brandon Garner. Composite execution commissioned under annotated
specification dated [X]. Source file purchased outright and held in archive."* That is
exactly how commissioned work has always operated: a painter's studio, an architect's
drafting team, a recording producer. Stating it converts the single largest credibility
vulnerability in the business into a provenance credential.

## New risks created by the correction

- ⚠️ **Confirm the outsourcer contract assigns commercial use and resale**, not merely
