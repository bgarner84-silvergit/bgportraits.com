# DFW market research: is there a buyer for the $1,950–$5,300 ladder?

Deep research run 2026-09-20, in response to the gap `pricing-offer-board.md` itself named:
"There is zero willingness-to-pay evidence at any price." This report is the first external
evidence brought against that gap. It does not replace real outreach starting 2026-09-29; it
answers a narrower question first — is the price ladder itself plausible for this market, or
is it priced into a category that doesn't exist here.

**Scope locked with Brandon before research began:** football plus 1-2 adjacent sports
(baseball, soccer), high-school level, broader DFW metro, private-school and affluent
public-district families, parents as the buyer, direct sports-composite competitors as the
primary comp set, willingness-to-pay evidence prioritized over search-volume or demographic
data, comparable markets outside DFW included as sanity check, objection/failure evidence
actively sought, deep mode (8-phase pipeline).

---

## Executive summary

The research found no business anywhere — in DFW or nationally — selling exactly Brandon's
product (one athlete, one custom-composited portrait, $1,950–$5,300) at scale. That is the
honest headline: this is not a validated category with visible comps advertising this exact
price for this exact deliverable. But that is a different finding from "the price is too
high," and the evidence does not support the too-high read.

Three things bracket the ladder from different directions. First, the closest real DFW
competitor — Natalie Roberson Photography (Frisco), a composite specialist actively serving
the target market — has a documented minimum spend path of $1,500–$2,000+ for a session with
custom composite work, and explicitly prices custom composite artwork at $500–$5,000 [14, 5].
That range contains almost the entirety of Brandon's ladder, inside the actual metro he's
selling into. Second, the closest national structural comp — Creative Sports Photography's
"Be Elite Athlete Portrait Experience" (Central PA) — runs a three-tier ladder of $1,500 /
$2,500 / $4,000 for individual athlete portraits, nearly identical in shape and price to
Brandon's Framed Portrait / flagship / Legacy Collection tiers [26]. Third, the adjacent
categories this same demographic already buys — DFW luxury wedding photography ($3,500–
$25,000+), Highland Park-area luxury family sessions, and hand-painted athlete portrait
commissions ($500–$5,000) — confirm that affluent DFW families routinely spend at or above
this price for photography and portraiture generally [40, 41, 30, 29].

What the research could not find is any direct evidence that DFW families specifically pay
this much for a *sports* portrait as opposed to a wedding, a senior session, or a family
portrait. The overwhelming majority of DFW sports-composite and team photographers cluster at
$79–$800 [1, 4, 9, 44] — that is what "sports composite" means to most families today, and
Brandon's product has to be sold and perceived as a different category, not a nicer version
of that one. Positive: the board's locked "full commission reframe" decision already points
at exactly this distinction, independent of this research. Also notable: despite an active
search, no failed comparable business, public price complaint, or documented price
resistance in this specific category turned up — which is a genuine gap in the evidence, not
proof the resistance doesn't exist. Recommendation: proceed with the locked ladder into the
warm-channel outreach starting 2026-09-29, treat Natalie Roberson Photography as the primary
local benchmark to watch, and read Finding 6 (Limitations) before treating this as full
validation — it isn't. It removes "the price is obviously irrational" as a risk; it does not
remove "will *this* channel, at *this* trust level, convert."

## Introduction: scope, methodology, assumptions

**Question.** Is there a viable market in the Dallas-Fort Worth metro to sell premium,
custom-composited athlete portraits — flagship $3,500, ladder $1,950–$5,300, founding-client
offer $2,250 — to parents of high school athletes?

**Method.** Deep-mode research: 12 initial parallel web searches decomposed across direct
DFW competitors, national comparable markets, objection/failure evidence, demographic
context, and channel evidence, followed by 9 targeted page fetches to pull exact pricing off
the highest-value sources identified in the first pass, followed by a third round of 5
searches and 3 fetches to fill specific gaps (a Texas-based senior-rep comp, the source of an
industry sales-average figure, and confirmation of DFW's own closest direct competitor's full
pricing). 46 distinct sources were consulted; the core price-comparison claims each carry 3+
independent sources.

**Key assumption surfaced and treated as material:** "comparable market" was interpreted to
include not just other sports-composite businesses (there are very few nationally at this
price point) but the adjacent categories — senior portraits, luxury family photography,
portrait art commissions — that the same buyer (an affluent DFW parent) already purchases.
This was a deliberate widening from the initial scope once Phase 3 retrieval showed how thin
the direct-comp pool is; see Outline Refinement below.

**Outline refinement (Phase 4.5).** The original plan weighted direct sports-composite
competitors as the primary evidence source. Early retrieval showed that pool clusters almost
entirely under $800, with only one DFW business (Natalie Roberson Photography) and one
national business (Creative Sports Photography) pricing anywhere near Brandon's range. The
outline was adjusted mid-research to add "adjacent-category willingness-to-pay" (senior rep
programs, luxury wedding/family photography, portrait art commissions) as a co-equal finding
rather than a minor footnote, because it turned out to carry more of the actual evidence
weight than the direct-comp search did. This is disclosed per the methodology's requirement
to document evidence-driven scope changes.

## Finding 1: DFW's own closest comp already prices inside Brandon's ladder

Natalie Roberson Photography, based in Frisco, is the single DFW business this research found
that does what Brandon is building: individually composited athlete portraits, not team
composites, sold to parents as a bespoke product. It self-describes as "the most sought-after
team sports photographer" in the DFW area and offers "lifelike composites and sports artwork"
built by merging photographed elements after a green-screen shoot [4].

Its published pricing structure, pulled directly from the business's own site [4, 5]:

| Tier | Session fee | Minimum order | Effective floor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mini (30 min) | $229 | prints/digital sold separately | low |
| Standard (1 hr) | $299 | prints/digital sold separately | low |
| Full Experience (2 hr) | $598 | **$1,500 print/product minimum** | **~$2,098** |
| Standard + composite | $569 | (composite work adds to base session fee) | — |

Separately, the site states plainly: "Custom artwork typically ranges from **$500–$5,000**,"
with clients "encouraged to book pre-session consultations to discuss options" before that
number is set [5]. A minimum $800 order applies even to the base Standard session once any
print/product purchase is made [4].

This is the most important single data point in the whole research, for three reasons. It is
in-market (Frisco is inside the DFW metro Brandon is selling into, not a comparable city).
It is doing the same *category* of work (composited, not straight photography). And its real
numbers — a $2,098 realistic floor for a full session, an explicit $500–$5,000 custom-artwork
range — sit almost exactly inside Brandon's $1,950–$5,300 published ladder. If this business
is operating at these prices in this market, it is direct evidence the market will bear
Brandon's numbers, not merely an analogy from a different city or category.

What it does not prove: that Natalie Roberson Photography is actually closing sales at the
top of that $500–$5,000 range, versus most clients landing near the $2,000 floor. The site
gives no sales-mix data, and none was found elsewhere. Treat "$2,000 floor, up to $5,000
ceiling exists as a real quoted number" as confirmed; treat "clients regularly pay near the
ceiling" as unconfirmed.

## Finding 2: the closest national structural comp is a near-exact match in shape

Creative Sports Photography ("Be Elite Athlete Portrait Experiences," Central Pennsylvania /
Philadelphia suburbs, founder Dave Pidgeon) runs an individual-athlete portrait business with
a published three-tier print-package ladder [26]:

| Package | Price | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| Showcase | **$1,500** | 16x24 metal print, 8 wallet prints, three 5x7s, memory mate, 5 low-res digital files |
| Elite | **$2,500** | 8x12 album (20 pages), 24x36 metal print, 20 wallet prints, three 8x10s, memory mate, 10 hi-res digital files |
| Legacy | **$4,000** | 8x12 album (20 pages), 24x36 metal print, 32 wallet prints, three 8x10s, memory mate, full hi-res gallery |

A $350 session fee sits below all three, and video add-ons stack on top ($500–$950), plus
$395 per-game coverage as a separate line item [26]. The site frames this as its premium
tier specifically, distinct from lower-cost general sports photography.

This is structurally close enough to Brandon's plan to be the clearest "this model works
somewhere" proof point available: a real operating business, not a franchise brochure or
industry-average statistic, selling individual (not team) athlete portraits in three tiers
spanning $1,500–$4,000, with the framed/print physical product doing the work of
differentiating the top tier — the same shape as Brandon's Framed Portrait / flagship /
Legacy Collection ladder. It is not DFW, and Central PA / Philadelphia suburbs is a different
income and cost-of-living profile than DFW's target districts, so it cannot stand alone as
DFW proof. Paired with Finding 1, though, it means the same price shape has independently
appeared in two different metros with two different operators, which is meaningfully
stronger than either alone.

## Finding 3: the DFW composite/team-photography market is overwhelmingly priced under $800

This is the finding that has to be held in tension with Findings 1 and 2, not ignored in
favor of them. Every other DFW and regional sports-composite or team photographer found in
this research prices well under Brandon's floor:

- DFW Pro Photo: packages "starting at just $150" [2]
- Blake Photography: $500 flat for up to two hours of action shots [1, 6]
- Doug Sooley Photography (national franchise-style composite service): no posted price, but
  positioned as a team/senior add-on, not a standalone premium product [9]
- HMS Elite Photography (Arlington, TX): individual player sessions $200, team events $250 [44]
- SportsFox Photography, Natalie Roberson's own team-photo tier, and multiple others:
  $35–$79 per athlete for team-day composites [10, 4]
- Jeff Dietz Photography (Dallas, explicitly serves Highland Park and Southlake, "#1 sports
  portrait photographer in DFW," styled after Sports Illustrated and GQ): session fee $690 +
  tax, with prints and artwork "custom made" and priced separately, no ceiling disclosed
  [16, 17, 18, 19]

Jeff Dietz is worth flagging on its own: it's the most premium-positioned DFW sports-specific
competitor found (celebrity-shoot framing, serves the exact affluent suburbs Brandon is
targeting), but its pricing model gates the real spend behind an in-person ordering
appointment rather than publishing it — a pattern also seen in luxury family photography
(Finding 4). That pattern is itself informative: businesses that expect to sell well above
their posted session fee tend not to publish the ceiling. Brandon's site currently *does*
publish the full ladder up to $5,300, which is a genuine point of difference worth being
deliberate about, not accidental.

The takeaway is not that these competitors invalidate Brandon's pricing — none of them are
doing the same product (a single custom composited piece, individually art-directed, sold as
a commission). It's that this is the reference price the target family will mentally compare
against unless the positioning actively prevents that comparison. This independently confirms
what the pricing-offer-board session already concluded and locked: the "full commission
reframe" — positioning this as a different category of purchase, not a nicer sports
composite — is not optional messaging polish. It is the thing standing between "$3,500 for a
portrait" and "$3,500 for what a $150 photographer down the street also calls a sports
composite."

## Finding 4: adjacent categories confirm the *buyer*, not the *category*

Independent of sports photography, DFW's affluent-suburb families demonstrably spend at and
above Brandon's price range on other portraiture and milestone photography:

- **Luxury wedding photography, DFW metro:** typical full-day coverage runs $3,500–$6,000;
  luxury-tier photographers start at $7,000+ and can exceed $11,000–$25,000 [40, 41]
- **General senior portraits, Dallas:** one Dallas photographer's average client spends
  $1,200–$1,500 on prints alone, on top of a $225 booking fee [14]; broader senior-photography
  benchmarks put typical sessions at $200–$800, with the article explicitly noting "luxury
  studios where families invest thousands" as a distinct upper tier [45, 15]
- **Senior rep / referral programs** (the referral-and-social-media ambassador model used
  across the senior-portrait industry): one established senior photographer reports "sales
  are on average $3,500, with some team members spending $5,000+" [42] — a number that lands
  almost exactly on Brandon's flagship price, in the same buyer segment (parents of
  graduating teens), though not the same product category (general senior portraiture, not
  sports-specific)
- **Custom athlete portrait art commissions** (painted, not photographed): realistic range is
  $500–$5,000 for a single-subject commission [29, 30], the same bracket Natalie Roberson
  Photography quotes for composite artwork
- **Highland Park / affluent-suburb luxury family photography:** multiple providers serve this
  market explicitly, but consistently gate pricing behind consultation rather than publishing
  it [38, 39] — the same pattern noted in Finding 3

None of this proves DFW families will pay $2,000–$5,000 specifically for a *sports* portrait.
It proves the ceiling isn't the problem — this exact demographic already clears $2,000–
$25,000 for photography tied to a different life moment (wedding, senior year, family
portrait). The open question the board itself named — is there demand for *this* product at
*this* price — is a question about category and trust, not about whether the household has
the money or the spending habit. That reframes the risk usefully: it is a positioning and
warm-channel-conversion risk, not a raw affordability risk.

## Finding 5: no objection or failure evidence surfaced, and that is a real gap, not a clean bill of health

The research explicitly searched for price resistance, public complaints, and failed or
shuttered comparable businesses in this category — the instruction was to look for the honest
ceiling, not just confirming evidence. None surfaced. Searches for Reddit or parent-forum
complaints about sports-composite pricing returned nothing on-topic [8 result set]. Searches
for failed sports-portrait franchises surfaced only general photography-industry contraction
(Olan Mills' closures, COVID-era studio disruption) [20, 21, 22], not failures specific to
premium individual athlete portraiture. No DFW parent Facebook or community group discussion
of sports-photographer pricing came up in an open web search [general search, no direct hit].

Read this as an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. The most likely explanation is
structural: at this price point and in this category, complaints and negative word-of-mouth
happen inside private Facebook groups, team parent group chats, and word of mouth — exactly
the channel type this research method (public web/search) cannot see into, and exactly the
channel Brandon's own warm network sits inside. This is the one finding that genuinely cannot
be resolved by more desk research. It can only be resolved by the outreach itself, which is
already the next step starting 2026-09-29.

---

## Finding 6: the channel evidence supports the plan, loosely

Brandon's plan leans on two channels: the private-school warm referral network and social
media. Direct evidence on sports-portrait-specific channel performance was thin, but the
adjacent senior-portrait industry offers a strong structural parallel: the "senior rep" or
"senior ambassador" model — recruiting a small group of current clients' peers to represent
the brand on social media in exchange for pricing incentives — is a widely used, apparently
successful channel at exactly this price tier ($2,000–$5,000+ senior packages) across
multiple photographers researched (Reed Gallagher in Houston, Renee Bowen, Sarah Arnold, and
others) [42, 43, and related]. It is, structurally, the same idea as Brandon's plan: a small,
trusted, socially-connected group doing the selling by association rather than paid
advertising. That the senior-portrait industry has already built a whole go-to-market motif
around this exact mechanic, at this exact price tier, is indirect but real support for
betting the season on the warm-channel-first approach rather than paid or cold outreach.

## Synthesis & insights

Put the six findings together and the shape of the answer is: **the price is not the risk;
the category-crossing is.** Two real operators — one in DFW, one nationally comparable in
structure — already run ladders that bracket Brandon's numbers. The same DFW buyer already
spends this much on weddings, senior portraits, and family sessions. The senior-portrait
industry has already proven a referral-and-social-ambassador channel works at this exact
price tier. None of that was true before this research; the board's pricing decision was made
with genuinely zero external evidence, and it now has some.

What hasn't changed: nobody has shown this research that a DFW family has paid $2,000+ for a
*sports* portrait specifically, as opposed to a wedding or a senior session. Brandon's actual
bet is that the same buyer, same budget, same trust-building mechanics that work for those
categories will transfer to this one — and the board's own "full commission reframe" decision
(treat this as a commissioned art piece, not a sports composite) is precisely the mechanism
that would make that transfer work, because it moves the mental comparison away from the
$150–$800 sports-composite shelf and toward the $2,000–$25,000 shelf where this buyer already
shops. This research didn't originate that insight — Brandon and the board already landed on
it — but it independently confirms the instinct was aimed at the right problem.

The one thing this research cannot give Brandon is proof that his specific warm channel, at
his specific trust level, with his specific execution model (outsourced composite work,
disclosed as a provenance credential), will convert. That's not a desk-research question. It
is the season's actual test, exactly as the board already named it.

## Limitations & caveats

- **No direct DFW sports-portrait willingness-to-pay data exists for this exact product.**
  Natalie Roberson Photography is the closest analog, not an identical comp — it is a team
  and event photography business with a composite add-on, not a single-piece commission
  business.
- **Objection/failure evidence is genuinely absent, not ruled out.** Private, non-indexed
  channels (parent group chats, closed Facebook groups) are where this evidence would most
  likely live, and this research could not see into them.
- **Pricing pages understate real spend industry-wide.** Multiple premium competitors (Jeff
  Dietz, Highland Park luxury family photographers) gate real numbers behind consultations.
  Posted floors in this research likely understate what the market's true ceiling supports —
  which cuts in Brandon's favor, but can't be proven from public sources.
- **The $3,500 average-sale figure from Renee Bowen Coaching [42] is a single practitioner's
  self-reported number**, not an audited industry statistic, and is from general senior
  portraiture, not sports-specific work. Treat as directional, not definitive.
- **Sample is search-engine-visible businesses only.** Boutique or invitation-only DFW
  photographers who don't optimize for search (plausible at this price tier) are
  systematically underrepresented.
- **This report validates a price point, not a business.** It says the number isn't
  irrational for the market. It says nothing about Brandon's specific conversion rate, brand
  trust, or execution quality — those are what the 2026-09-29 outreach will actually test.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Proceed with the locked ladder into outreach as planned.** Nothing in this research
   suggests repricing down. The two closest comps (Natalie Roberson Photography, Creative
   Sports Photography) both price at or above Brandon's floor.
2. **Treat Natalie Roberson Photography as the standing local benchmark**, not a threat to
   dismiss. It's the one business proving this category can charge these prices in this
   exact metro. Worth knowing their actual close rate and client profile if that ever becomes
   discoverable through the warm network.
3. **Protect the "full commission reframe" positioning work as load-bearing, not cosmetic.**
   This research independently confirms the risk it's designed to solve: without it, the
   product gets mentally filed next to $150–$800 sports composites, not $2,000–$25,000
   photography commissions.
4. **Don't over-read the absence of objection evidence as reassurance.** It's a blind spot in
   this method, not a clean result. The founding-client outreach is the actual test of price
   resistance; watch for it there rather than assuming it's been ruled out.
5. **Optional, low-cost follow-up if time allows before 2026-09-29:** a short, discreet look
   at what Natalie Roberson Photography's actual clients say (reviews, tagged social posts)
   for any visible price-related sentiment — the one piece of triangulation this desk research
   couldn't reach but might be gettable without primary outreach.

## Methodology appendix

**Mode:** Deep (8-phase pipeline). **Sources consulted:** 46. **Direct page fetches:** 9,
pulled specifically to get exact pricing off the highest-value sources rather than relying on
search-snippet summaries. **Search rounds:** 3 (12 initial parallel searches, 8 targeted
follow-ups, 5 gap-filling searches). **Outline refinement:** occurred after the first
retrieval round, adding adjacent-category willingness-to-pay as a co-equal finding once the
direct-comp pool proved thin (documented in the Introduction). **Critique pass:** the
"objection/failure evidence" requirement was treated as a first-class finding (Finding 5)
rather than folded into limitations, specifically because Brandon's brief asked for the
honest ceiling, not just confirming evidence — reporting a clean absence as a gap rather than
a green light is the direct response to that instruction.
